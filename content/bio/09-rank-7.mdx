I really didn't deserve to be in the single digits the way most students who end up there do. Usually you have to already be awake before the competition starts. The future valedictorian knows freshman year matters. The kid optimizing for selective admissions is tracking GPA weighting and course rigor while everybody else is still discovering where the bathrooms are. I was mostly just doing my best because that was what I did. Mrs. Snook talked about how lazy our class was—[verify wording/context]—and apparently the local competition was forgiving enough that ordinary overperformance kept pushing me upward before I even understood there was a leaderboard worth watching.

Then sometime around junior year I became aware of my rank in a psychologically real way. Not "class rank exists," but THERE IS A NUMBER ATTACHED TO ME AND OTHER PEOPLE HAVE NUMBERS NEXT TO IT. Once the measurement became visible, I became competitive. Of course I did. This is one of my recurring vulnerabilities: give me a legible objective function and I can become extremely stupid about maximizing it. Suddenly the marginal assignment mattered differently. A grade was not just evidence I understood the course; it was movement or protection in a ranking system. The students immediately around me became reference points. I studied harder. I cared about tiny differences that would have been meaningless before the metric acquired social reality.

By senior year the utility of school performance had collapsed toward preserving the rank. I had already learned enormous amounts and genuinely loved some subjects, especially math and science, but there was also this almost mechanical optimization running over the top: do the thing that keeps the number where I want it. Most people looking at rank seven saw optionality. Good student. Engineering scholarship. College. Career. The whole conventional pipeline had opened. From inside my JW model, however, the ranking had a very different terminal value. It showed that I COULD pursue that world. Which meant walking away from it could become a more meaningful sacrifice.

That is such a dangerous sentence in retrospect. But at seventeen it made perfect sense.
