Two books in one: a first-person account of Auschwitz, and the clinical theory
Frankl derived from it. The theory is only credible because of the account.

The central claim is that the last human freedom — the choice of one's attitude
in any given set of circumstances — cannot be taken, and that meaning is found
rather than manufactured: in work, in love, and in the stance one takes toward
unavoidable suffering. Frankl is careful that this is not optimism. He is
explicit that the best did not survive.

It sits next to [Psalm 88](/readings/psalm-88) in my head for the same reason.
Both refuse to make suffering instrumental, and both insist there is something
left to do inside it anyway.
