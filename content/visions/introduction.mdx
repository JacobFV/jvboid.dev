I love science and engineering and people. That sentence is true, but on its own it reads like everyone else's bio, so here is the longer version.

## Where I come from

I was raised a Jehovah's Witness in Texas. It was the center of my identity from birth until I was 24. It gave me real things — a family that valued learning and kindness, a deep instinct for justice, the habit of taking ideas seriously enough to reorganize my life around them. It also kept me apart from the world on purpose. No parties, no dating, very few friends my own age, and at 17 — top-ten student, full engineering scholarship in hand — I turned down college because the people I trusted most told me it was worldly. I spent the next several years in full-time ministry instead.

I know from the inside what it's like to live entirely within a story, to sacrifice real things for it, and then to watch it come apart. Leaving cost me my community, a decade of ordinary experiences most people get for free, and eventually my belief in God. What it left behind is a permanent interest in how belief systems hold people, why people cling to hope even when they suspect it's false, and what it actually takes to walk out of one — questions I now think about mostly through the lens of AI, incentives, and ideology.

The mind kept working the whole time, though. The reason I was always so deep into math as a teenager was that it seemed so beautiful how it could be so *true* — I found that really beautiful, and I still do. In high school I'd loaded up on community college credit — Calculus I through III and differential equations in my junior and senior years — and from 2018 to 2020 I read arXiv papers in the back room of the McDonald's where I worked. So when the door to college finally opened, I finished my CS degree at UT Arlington in two years, taking the maximum course load every semester. Make of that what you will about how I operate when I'm finally allowed to run.

## The compressed decade

Because the door opened late, everything arrived at once. My first "tech-bro" job was in LA in fall–winter 2024, and there — as I'm sure many people arriving in the film industry have discovered before me — I opened my eyes to what the tech industry actually is, and how much less exciting that is than the story. Losing that story sent me re-examining everything else I believed, and that's when I woke up from the JW indoctrination too. A few months later I made the call to put down the dog I'd grown up with. Then I moved to San Francisco, and the cliff at China Beach was the climax of all that accumulated worthlessness: I didn't fall by accident. I believed my life was worthless and that there was no hope left, and I deliberately put myself in that situation. I dropped the first ten feet and caught a ledge I could feel was about to give, and hanging there is where I made the promise: if I made it through the remaining forty feet, I would actually live. I did — torn up, in shock, carried out by helicopter — and I'm still keeping it.

I call this period my "second adolescence" — developmentally doing at 24–26 what most people do at 15–19, including the awkward parts, the crazy stuff, and the parts I'd do differently now. I dump and reflect on my unfiltered thoughts on my reddit anon. I've been depressed for long stretches of it. I've done therapy, I've done 80-hour work weeks as anesthesia, I've done the 2am existential spiral about death and meaning more times than I can count. I'm not going to pretend that's resolved. It's an active project, like everything else on this site.

[The Shape of Experience](https://theshapeofexperience.org/) is my long-form attempt to treat conscious experience as the interior of a self-maintaining dynamical system — and what matters about it isn't my autobiography. If experience has real structure, then the human experience has real meaning, and we can engineer it — and that hope is for all of us. The pieces: [a geometry of affect](https://theshapeofexperience.org/part-2/the-geometry-of-affect) where emotions are trajectories on a viability manifold rather than mysterious qualia, [aesthetics as the modulation of affect through form](https://theshapeofexperience.org/part-3/aesthetics-the-modulation-of-affect-through-form), [affect engineering](https://theshapeofexperience.org/part-3/affect-engineering-technologies-of-experience) as the technology that follows, and [the inescapability of selfhood](https://theshapeofexperience.org/part-3/the-expression-of-inevitability-human-responses-to-inescapable-selfhood) as the existential condition all of culture is responding to. The meaning crisis I lived through — the one that put me on that cliff — appears there as a specific geometric collapse in affect space, which is exactly what it felt like.

The far end of that project is the question I can't put down: what does a life inscribe into the world, and [can an echo become a voice again](/posts/can-an-echo-become-a-voice-again)? Very few marks a person leaves endure long after them. I was raised on the promise of resurrection; I've replaced the promise with [a research question](https://theshapeofexperience.org/part-5/inscription-and-re-entry) — identity as an attractor carved into the causal graph, and re-entry as something to be formalized and tested rather than either worshipped or sneered at.

## What I'm building

I'm building [commandAGI](https://commandagi.com). The thesis is simple to state: intelligence should not remain trapped in language. Most AI today is a text interface over opaque computation — it looks smart because a human is silently supplying the eyes, hands, memory, and liability. commandAGI is my attempt to make that missing substrate explicit: a command layer for real environments — computers, browsers, cameras, robots, simulations, and real-world services — where every agent has perception, memory, permissions, and an accountable event log, and every claim is tied to evidence. Long-term it extends to the exocortex (a memory and perception scaffold around the person), structured latent dynamics instead of brute-force scale (so agency gets cheap enough to decentralize), and reciprocal transparency — the systems that observe you should be inspectable back. Make the world inspectable. Make agency cheap. Make hidden systems answerable.

And let me be clear about the why: science and tech don't entertain me anymore the way they did when I was a teenager. I see them now as the highest-leverage tools for modifying the incentive structures constraining the human condition — and *that* faith gives me hope. I lost a guaranteed future when I left my religion — the paradise, the resurrection, the whole settled cosmology — and I've chosen to spend my life trying to build some of what was promised instead of grieving that it was never real.

Along the way I also ran [Go Human Fund](https://gohuman.fund/) (2023–2025), a personally financed micro-seed fund that wrote the smallest first checks — about $100 — to 20+ founders across six countries, at the moment when they had nothing but an idea and needed someone to say *I see you, go*. It's closed now, mission complete, and the founders keep building. That's the kind of mark I mean.

Résumé facts, for completeness: most recently API/Integration Architect at [AGI, Inc.](https://agi.app), working on APIs, integrations, and agent infrastructure for on-device mobile AI agents. Before that: Breezy, Deepshard (Truffle AI Computer), Motio, and UTA research labs. BS Computer Science from UT Arlington, 3x AWS certified, TensorFlow Developer certified. Started programming VB6 at age 10 and never really stopped — Python, TypeScript, Rust, cloud infrastructure, ML, hardware, whatever the problem needs.
